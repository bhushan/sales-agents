Write three cold emails from a {{seller}} salesperson to the person
in the pack.

Bottom line up front. Each email says its point in the first sentence
and spends the rest of itself backing that point up. The reader has to
be able to stop after line one and still know why the email exists.

Every email runs in this order:
1. The bottom line. One sentence: what is now true for this person,
   in the terms of the job they are measured on. No greeting line
   that carries meaning, no warm-up, nothing before it.
2. The evidence. The pack fields that make line 1 true, most specific
   first: what this person said or did, then what changed at their
   company, then what changed in their industry.
3. The ask. One question or one offer, and nothing after it.

The subject line is the bottom line, compressed. Not a teaser for it,
not a topic, not a question.

For the evidence, use the most specific thing the pack holds:
- if "The person" records what they said or did, use that, quoted or
  paraphrased accurately
- otherwise use what changed at their company, then their industry
Do not flatter. Pointing at what someone said is research. Praising
them for saying it is filler.

Hard rules:
1. Every sentence either states what a pack field states, or draws a
   consequence from named pack fields. Nothing else goes in.
2. The bottom line is a consequence sentence. It rests on pack fields
   and nothing else, and the trace table has to name every one of
   them. A bottom line you cannot trace is a guess about the account.
3. Nothing from the "Do not use" section. Not reworded either.
4. No sentence that would be true of any company in this industry. A
   bottom line that survives swapping the account name is not one.
5. Never write any of these. They read as safe and are invented:
   - other customers, peers, or "companies like yours"
   - any comparison or ranking the pack does not make
   - what the account lacks or has not done, unless the pack says so
   - what the account has, owns, tracks or is planning internally,
     unless the pack says so. You cannot see inside the company.
   - a subject line stating a fact the pack does not hold
   - a number, date or name that is not in the pack
6. A question may only take for granted something the pack states.
   "Who owns X today?" is inventing X unless the pack has X.
7. Fields marked "(inferred, not graded)" are usable, but a sentence
   built on one is a read of the situation, not a fact. Write it as
   one.
8. Under 120 words each. Subject lines under 8 words.
9. Banned phrases: "I hope this finds you well", "quick question",
   "circling back", "just following up".

Email 1: Bottom line is what the change means for their job. Evidence
  is the most specific thing the pack holds about them. Ask one
  question. Do not describe {{seller}}'s features.
Email 2: A different bottom line, not a reminder of email 1's. Take
  it from what changed at their company and what this person is
  measured on. Mention email 1 in one clause at most.
Email 3: Short. The bottom line is the one thing worth knowing even
  if they never reply, and the ask is an easy no.

Before you return each email, read its first sentence on its own. If
it does not carry the point, it is an opener and not a bottom line:
rewrite it and move what you wrote down into the evidence.

After each email, a table: sentence → the pack field it came from.
For a consequence sentence, name every field it rests on.
